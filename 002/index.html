02. Meaning of the Front Page

What sits on the
front page?

The front page contains a selection of the day's most important stories. It acts as the visual and editorial introduction to the whole newspaper.

"Its selection and arrangement determine how readers notice, understand and consume information."
Masthead

The newspaper's name, displayed prominently at the top — its strongest identity element.

Headline & Subheadline

Clear, accurate, concise, relevant — summarising and explaining the story.

Lead Story

The most prominent report; its position, size and treatment show its importance.

Photos & Captions

Visual information with context; properly captioned and ethically published.

Body Text

Readable, properly columned reporting with byline and dateline.

Briefs & Index

News summaries and page references guiding readers inside.

Advertisements

Where editorial and commercial policy allows, they occupy part of the page.

Date & Edition

Date, edition and price identify the exact issue of record.

04. Principles of Newspaper Layout

Balance, proportion,
contrast & hierarchy

A newspaper layout should combine editorial priorities with visual clarity — principles that apply as much to digital pages as to print.

"White space is not empty — it gives the news room to breathe and makes elements more prominent."
— Layout Principle
Balance

Distribute visual weight evenly through placement of headlines, photos, text and space.

Proportion

Size follows importance — the main story earns the larger headline.

Contrast

Size, weight, images, colour and white space make key elements stand out.

Alignment

A consistent alignment system creates order and improves readability.

Unity

All elements should belong to one visual composition.

White Space & Hierarchy

Space prevents overcrowding; hierarchy orders what readers notice first.

Layout PrinciplesBalance / Contrast / Hierarchy

05. Types of Newspaper Layout

Seven ways to
compose a page

Different newspapers adopt different approaches — each with its own character, strengths and risks.

"Modular design is the workhorse of the modern page — easy to organise, easy to scan."
Symmetrical

Even distribution around a central structure — formal and orderly.

Asymmetrical

Unequal elements, overall balance — dynamic and contemporary.

Modular

Rectangular blocks — easy to organise; modern design's standard.

Horizontal

Elements flow across the width — strong visual movement.

Vertical

Top-to-bottom arrangement — clear sequence for many stories.

Circus

Many elements and sizes — energetic, but excess reduces clarity.

Contrast

Big differences in size and type create deliberate emphasis.

Layout TypesFormal → Dynamic

06. The Front-Page Layout Process

From news selection to
final production

Designing the front page is a structured editorial workflow, not decoration after the fact.

STEP 1

Select the News

Judge by news value, interest, timeliness, proximity, impact, prominence, conflict, human interest.

STEP 2

Set Priority

Decide which story receives the greatest prominence.

STEP 3

Write Headlines

Prepare them to the available space and importance of each story.

STEP 4

Choose Photographs

Relevant, high-quality images where appropriate.

STEP 5

Plan the Page

Sketch placement of headlines, stories, photos and elements.

STEP 6

Select Typography

Fonts and sizes from the newspaper's design system.

STEP 7

Place Content

Arrange stories, images and elements to the plan.

STEP 8–9

Proof & Produce

Check spelling, facts, captions, alignment; prepare for print or digital publication.

SELECT → PRIORITISE → HEADLINE → PHOTOGRAPH → PLAN → SET TYPE → PLACE → PROOF → PRODUCE

07. Typography in Newspaper Design

Type is the
voice of the page

Typography covers typefaces, sizes, weights, spacing and line lengths. Headlines are larger and heavier; body text must stay comfortable across long reads; subheadings divide long stories into sections.

For Indian-language newspapers, typography is especially important — the design must support the specific characteristics of each script while keeping readability.

"Different type sizes and weights tell readers what to read first."
THE DAILY CHRONICLE
TYPE SPECIMENPAGE ONE₹5.00
HEADLINE — LARGEST & HEAVIEST
Subheading — italic serif, smaller, explains the headline

Body text is set smaller again, in narrow justified columns that stay comfortable to read. Subheadings divide the story into sections, and captions explain the photographs.

The hierarchy of sizes — masthead, headline, subhead, body, caption — creates the order in which readers notice information.

Masthead → Headline → Subhead → Body → Caption

08–10. Photography · Infographics · Colour

The visual
journalism toolkit

A strong news photograph communicates instantly; infographics explain complexity; colour — once rare — now supports branding, section identity and highlight boxes.

"Colour should support communication rather than simply decorate the page."
Photography

Infographics

Charts, maps, diagrams, timelines and data graphics for elections, budgets, sports, science and health — accurate and clearly sourced.

Colour

Used for branding, headlines, section identification and highlight boxes — but excess colour makes a page cluttered.

11. Contemporary Newspaper Layout Trends

Cleaner, bigger,
modular

Competition from television, websites and social media has reshaped the printed page — without abandoning the old disciplines.

11.1Cleaner Layouts

More white space, fewer decorations — less visual clutter.

11.2Larger Headlines

Strong hierarchy that attracts attention at a distance.

11.3Dominant Photography

Large photographs as major front-page elements.

11.4Modular Design

Clearly defined blocks, easier to scan, flexible to build.

11.5Infographics

Explanatory graphics for complex stories.

11.6Visual Storytelling

Photos, graphics, headlines and summaries combined.

11.7Shorter Text Blocks

Summaries, bullets and subheads for scanning readers.

11.8Strong Hierarchy

Clear difference between major and minor stories.

11.9Consistent Branding

One visual identity across print, web, apps and social.

11.10Digital Integration

QR codes, website references and multimedia links in print.

11.11Data Journalism

Charts, maps and visualisations for data-driven reporting.

11.12Responsive Design

Planning how stories appear across phones, tablets, computers and print.

13–14. Visual Hierarchy · The Designer's Role

Attention is
designed

Readers never give equal attention to every element. Designers direct the eye using size, position, typography, colour, images and white space.

The designer works between editorial content and visual presentation — understanding news priorities, maintaining balance and identity, ensuring readability, and checking the final page. Design is part of the communication process, not decoration.

The Designer's Checklist
01Understand the news priorities
02Arrange content effectively
03Maintain visual balance & identity
04Apply typography consistently
05Ensure readability to the last proof
Size

Larger elements receive greater visual attention.

Position

Prominent areas attract the eye first.

Type & Colour

Bold headlines and colour distinguish elements.

Images

Striking photographs become focal points.

White Space

Space around an element makes it more prominent.

Order

Hierarchy defines the sequence of reading.

15. Common Problems in Newspaper Layout

What good design
avoids

Poor design undermines even strong journalism — clutter, weak hierarchy and bad type cost readers and credibility.

"If every headline looks equally important, readers may not know where to begin."
Overcrowding

Too much information makes a page difficult to read.

Poor Hierarchy

Equal-looking headlines leave readers lost.

Excessive Decoration

Unnecessary elements distract from the news.

Poor Typography

Small or inappropriate fonts reduce readability.

Weak Images

Irrelevant or low-quality photographs weaken stories.

Inconsistent Alignment

Misalignment makes the page appear disorganised.

Excessive Colour

Too many colours create visual confusion.

The Fix

Clarity, hierarchy, restraint, consistent type and honest imagery.

Conclusion

Design that helps readers
understand what matters

A successful front page combines editorial judgment with balance, proportion, contrast, alignment, white space and hierarchy. Contemporary design moves toward cleaner layouts, larger photographs, modular structures and digital integration — but design must never replace journalism's purpose: communicating important information clearly, accurately and responsibly.

Elements — masthead, headlines, lead story, photographs, captions, bylines and supporting stories.

Principles — balance, proportion, contrast, alignment, unity, white space and hierarchy.

Trends — modular design, infographics, data visualisation and integration with digital platforms.

"Design well so the news is understood — not merely noticed."
